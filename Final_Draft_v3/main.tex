\begin{abstract}
Respiratory sound classification usually relies on a single public benchmark and one headline score. This project investigates the hidden details behind that metric. Breathing cycles are segmented and converted to log-scaled spectrograms. A lightweight convolutional neural network and three pretrained vision transformers are trained on the official patient disjoint partition. Evaluation covers standard classification metrics, parameter count, and training time. 

The CNN achieves the highest challenge score of 0.5602. The top transformer requires 12 times the parameters but scores 3 points lower. Another transformer simply predicts the majority class, gaining high raw accuracy only because normal sounds make up 59 percent of the corpus. Spectrogram masking improves the CNN by 4 points but offers no benefit to the transformers. Grouping the classes into a binary abnormal versus normal task increases the score by 7 points. Most model errors happen when distinguishing crackles from wheezes, a task where human physician agreement is under 0.40.

The best model is evaluated further using a twelve-row ablation study, noise floor testing, and explainable artificial intelligence. Testing on the 47-patient partition shows major differences between cycle-level and patient-level analyses. The overall performance ceiling comes from the dataset labels and sample size rather than the network architecture.

\added{The frozen best model is then run over two unseen corpora, SPRSound and HF\_Lung\_V1. Its score falls to 0.40 and 0.49 against an always-Normal floor of 0.50, while a probe fitted on its frozen features reaches 0.74 on SPRSound: the representation transfers and the fitted decision boundary does not. Confidence rises as accuracy falls, and on a phonocardiogram corpus used as a negative control the model is more confident about heart sounds than about its own test data and does not reject them at better than chance.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Physicians usually reach for a stethoscope first to examine patients with respiratory conditions. Heartbeats and background noises often hide important breathing sounds like crackles and wheezes. Diagnoses vary a lot from one doctor to another because of these listening challenges. The ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database~\cite{rocha2019icbhi} serves as a standard tool to help automate this detection process using machine learning. This project selects this problem because the benchmark is mature. The objective is not to create a completely new architecture. The goal is to ask different questions about the testing process. The project investigates whether the scores reported by the field on this dataset actually mean what people assume they mean.

\subsection{Literature Review}
\label{subsec:lit}

Yu \emph{et al.}~\cite{electronics2025review} have reviewed 135 technical publications
built on this database. They separated the tasks into two. To recognise the patient's
pathological state, where many systems exceed 98 percent accuracy. To categorise the
adventitious sound in a single cycle. Severe class imbalance makes the second task much
more difficult. They suggest using the data surrounding the audio more effectively and
creating models that are easier to understand. Recent work is driven in that direction.
Since around 2021, progress has come more from improved pre-training than from improved
architecture. On the official challenge metric~\cite{respirenet2021}, convolutional
systems pretrained on ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet} reach a plateau around 0.56.
Transformers that have been pretrained on AudioSet~\cite{gemmeke2017audioset} have
scores that are five to nine points higher~\cite{bae2023patchmix,bts2024,pafa2025}. Suma
\emph{et al.}~\cite{suma2025mtl} use a single-input, multi-output network over
mel-frequency cepstral coefficients to simultaneously address both tasks. Four
backbones are compared. MobileNet does the best, scoring 91 percent on diseases and 74
percent on sounds. Which partition those numbers are on is never mentioned. That is the
initial indication of the issue this paper addresses. Gains of about one point are
reported by two 2025 fusion systems~\cite{adffnet2025,bspc2025fusion}, a scale at which
the following measurement questions become significant.

Bae \emph{et al.}~\cite{bae2023patchmix} fine-tuned an Audio Spectrogram
Transformer~\cite{gong2021ast} with a patch-mixing contrastive objective and achieved
0.6237. Niizumi \emph{et al.}~\cite{frozenfm2025} froze the audio foundation model
entirely, trained only a linear probe, and still achieved 0.5938. That is above several
fully fine-tuned convolutional systems. Most of the useful information sits in the
pretrained representation, not the fine-tuning, provided the pretraining was on audio.
Moummad and Farrugia~\cite{scl2023} achieved 0.5755 using a small CNN and contrastive
pretraining on metadata. Nguyen and Pernkopf~\cite{nguyen2022cotuning} add that the
transfer from ImageNet to spectrograms is fragile. It needs regularisation. Kim
\emph{et al.}~\cite{kim2025metadata} pushed the metadata idea furthest. They came to a
decision that we had to face head-on. They assessed the sources of domain shift,
including age, sex, chest position, and recording equipment. A supervised contrastive
objective was guided by the most informative pairings, and the main limiting factor for
models trained on this corpus was found to be recording-condition bias. The results of
our search for that axis in the actual data are presented in
Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}.

To examine the reasons behind a prediction, concept bottleneck
models~\cite{koh2020cbm} filter it using human-defined concepts. This is appealing
since wheezes and crackles have physical definitions. This was our original plan.
Karim \emph{et al.}~\cite{karim2024mosquito} combined vision transformers with OpenMax
rejection. We reimplemented that as our open-set baseline. Knowledge distillation with
a teaching assistant~\cite{pavel2024kd} gave us the compression arm. Yang \emph{et
al.}~\cite{yang2024ood} has done the vocabulary separating out-of-distribution
detection from open-set recognition. Kejriwal \emph{et al.}~\cite{kejriwal2024owl} has
done the staged open-world protocol. Cho and Lee~\cite{cho2025openset} are the only
group found to have performed open-set recognition on respiratory audio. They reported
an improvement over a semi-supervised baseline, not an absolute score. Cruz \emph{et
al.}~\cite{cruz2025owl} surveyed what the open-world field still has not solved. They
group the open problems into three areas. The theory of novelty, the design of agents,
and their evaluation. The third area aligns with us. They name inconsistent evaluation
metrics as an unresolved issue.

Tzeng \emph{et al.}~\cite{tzeng2025jmir} had seven senior physicians blindly annotate a
quarter of the ICBHI test set. On clean audio, they reached an ICBHI score of 47.77
percent, with a sensitivity of 23.23 percent, and a mean self-rated confidence of 2.88
out of 5. Melbye \emph{et al.}~\cite{melbye2016kappa} had twelve physicians classify
twenty reference recordings. The agreement fell below $\kappa = 0.40$ for detailed
descriptions. It rose to 0.62 and 0.59 when crackles and wheezes were collapsed to a
simple presence. Huang \emph{et al.}~\cite{huang2024crackles} report a larger clinical
archive with agreement on wheezes being very high ($\kappa = 0.948$) and agreement on
crackle being only moderate ($\kappa = 0.516$). The three studies disagree on the
level. They do agree on the shape, that crackle labels are far less reliable than
wheeze labels. As the description becomes more detailed, reliability declines
dramatically. Bikku \emph{et al.}~\cite{scirep2025cnnrnn} combined a recurrent-convolutional 
encoder over mel-spectrograms from two corpora. They associate wheezes
with obstructive disease and crackles with pneumonia. They report 94.0 percent accuracy
under five-fold cross-validation and use gradient, Shapley, and local surrogate methods
to explain the result. The explanations align with the same cycle labels that the three
studies characterized earlier. The partition is not the official one. The number
cannot be read against the systems in the first block. Both observations shaped how we
report our own interpretability results in Section~\ref{subsec:xai}.

\subsection{Gaps in the Related Work}
\label{subsec:gaps}

Reading the two groups side by side exposed four gaps we could act on.

The reported score is treated as a characteristic of the model. Papers from the first
group report their composite numbers and compare them with those of other papers. A
few of them state the circulating definition they used. The description of the
partition is often vague or non-existent. The multi-task study does not name any.
Five-fold cross-validation is reported in the convolutional-recurrent study, which
can't be compared to the official split figures next to it. We could not find an ICBHI
paper that ablates its evaluation protocol the way it ablates its architecture. Our own
audit shows the protocol is worth more than any architectural choice we made.

The official split is assumed to be independent of the patient, but it is not. The
published file assigns recordings, not patients. Two patients have recordings on both
sides. That is a validity fault that no paper we read mentions.

The reference standard is never characterized. The first group validates models
against ICBHI's cycle labels. The second shows that those labels are reproduced by
trained physicians at only 47.77 percent, with agreement ($\kappa$) well below 0.40 for
fine distinctions. A detector scored against a reference standard of unknown
reliability produces a number of unknown meanings.

We could not find a paper that set a validity threshold in advance, ran a test against it, and reported a failure. Without that, a reader cannot tell how many attempts a reported success survived.

\subsection{Our Work}
\label{subsec:ourwork}

We started with an open-world multi-task design: a shared spectrogram backbone, one head for
the four sound-event classes, one head for disease, and a bottleneck of physics-derived
acoustic concepts in between. Before building the bottleneck we fixed a threshold. The
extractors had to reproduce ICBHI's own crackle and wheeze labels at an area under the curve of
at least 0.65, with an interval that excludes chance, read once per run on the test partition,
and we budgeted two runs. Crackle reached 0.5506 and 0.5580. Wheeze reached 0.5729 and
0.5340. The rule said stop, and we stopped.

Looking for a reason sent us into our own evaluation code, where we found five faults.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} lists them. Correcting them moved our headline number from 0.7077
to 0.5602 on the same model and the same code. That gap of 0.1475 is larger than ImageNet
pretraining, larger than augmentation, and larger than the spread between our best and worst
backbone. What we deliver is a measurement rather than a mechanism: a corrected protocol, the
baselines it produces, and the checks that let somebody else catch each fault in their own
pipeline.

We rebuilt the model side under that protocol. One ImageNet-pretrained convolutional network
and three ImageNet-pretrained vision
transformers~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit,liu2021swin,touvron2021deit} were trained on identical
inputs with identical schedules, each once clean and once with
SpecAugment~\cite{park2019specaugment}. A fourth transformer, an Audio Spectrogram
Transformer~\cite{gong2021ast} pretrained on AudioSet, was trained before the audit and stored
no prediction table, so its challenge-metric score cannot be recovered.
Section~\ref{subsec:ast} reports that gap instead of repairing it, because it is our own
argument landing on the one model the literature says should have won.

The best model, MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} with SpecAugment, went through a
twelve-row ablation, a three-seed noise floor, paired patient-level tests, and gradient and
occlusion attribution~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}. Every transformer lost to the convolutional
baseline and the largest collapsed to the majority class. We report that instead of tuning
until it stops being true. The attribution argues against our own score: with a wheeze present
the model attends to the wheeze band less than when it is absent, by 0.0534 at $p = 0.001$, and
that measurement is what put the zero-padding row into the ablation.

The contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item We quantify the evaluation protocol as an ablatable pipeline component on ICBHI 2017,
reporting a five-fault protocol ablation that moves an unchanged MobileNetV2 checkpoint from
0.5602 to 0.7077 ($\Delta$ 0.1475), exceeding the combined effect of ImageNet pretraining
(0.0603), spectrogram masking (0.0402) and backbone choice.

\item We benchmark the reference standard itself with a blinded single-physician study (132
clips, 6 calibration exemplars, 12 hidden duplicates), reproducing a published seven-physician
crackle sensitivity at 23.1 percent against 23.23 percent, and measure a paired $+0.169$ AUROC
advantage for a frozen audio probe over that physician on identical clips (wheeze $p = 0.046$;
crackle undecided at $p = 0.087$).

\item We identify checkpoint selection as a first-class pipeline component, measuring a 0.0752
to 0.1015 penalty across three seeds for minimum-loss selection against challenge-score
selection, and scope the measurement to a shared monitoring partition so that only the
difference between the two rules is claimed.

\item We document a patient leak in the published 60/40 split (patients 156 and 218 on both
sides), price its repair at 0.0039, and separate validity from inflation by re-testing ten
ablation rows at both units of analysis (7 of 10 significant per cycle, 1 of 10 per patient, 6
verdict flips over 2,636 cycles from 47 patients).

\item We report corrected-protocol baselines for four backbones trained under one schedule
(MobileNetV2, Swin-T, DeiT-S and ViT-B/16, each clean and masked), gated by a three-seed
admissibility floor of 0.0141 that marks 3 of 11 ablation deltas unresolved. Every
ImageNet-pretrained transformer loses to the 2.23~M-parameter convolutional baseline, and
ViT-B/16 (85.8~M) collapses to the majority class.

\item We pre-register a concept-validity gate (AUROC $\geq 0.65$ with a DeLong interval
excluding chance, two-run budget), report its failure at 0.5580 on crackle and 0.5340 on wheeze,
and retract our own label-noise explanation using a pre-registered frozen-AudioSet probe that
reaches 0.7115 and 0.7621 on the identical cycles.



\item We quantify attribution against the score rather than illustrating it, measuring a
$-0.0534$ band-pointing deficit when wheeze is present ($p = 0.001$) and a 0.098 mean
attribution correlation across identical tiled copies of one cycle, the second of which
motivated a zero-padding ablation row costing 0.0311.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset}
We use the ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database~\cite{rocha2019icbhi} from its public release. It includes 920 recordings from 126 patients, annotated per respiratory cycle with a start time, an end time, a crackle flag, and a wheeze flag. Combining the two flags gives us the four sound-event classes used throughout. They are labeled as Normal, Crackle, Wheeze, and Both across 6,898 annotated cycles, with a median length of 2.42~s. We standardize that to an 8.0~s network input. Recordings were captured with four stethoscopes at several chest locations and at mixed sample rates. We resample it all to 16~kHz mono.


Two structural facts about the corpus shaped the project and are worth stating before any results. Each patient carries a single diagnosis label. Those labels are extremely imbalanced: COPD alone covers 64 of the 126 patients, 83.3 percent of all cycles. That is why the disease side of our original two-head design was eventually dropped from the headline. Only four patients (112, 158, 218, and 226) had multiple device records. Patient identification and device identity are almost perfectly confounded. It is important to note that recording-condition bias is the primary limiting factor for models trained here, according to Kim \emph{et al.}~\cite{kim2025metadata}. They demonstrate the importance of an axis that this corpus cannot measure internally. Separating the device from the patient would require that patients be recorded on several devices, but there are only four. It is a reason to take their cross-dataset approach seriously, not a reason to doubt it.

Table~\ref{tab:partitions} presents the three partitions used in this paper, along with the class composition of each. The published split file assigns recordings, not patients. Patients 156 and 218 have recordings on both sides. Our corrected partition moves every recording of a leaking patient into the training side. That is the conservative direction. The test side is guaranteed to contain no patient the model has trained on. The repair costs 12 of 381 test recordings. It moves the ratio from 58.6/41.4 to 59.9/40.1, marginally closer to the nominal 60/40 than the published file itself. All new results in this paper are on the corrected partition. The other two partitions appear only where a comparison demands it, always by name

On the corrected test partition, both classes hold 86 cycles against 1,560 Normal. This shows how severe the imbalance is. That is why every model here trains with an inverse-frequency weighted loss (\ref{eq:weights}). It is also why accuracy is a misleading headline on this corpus: always predicting Normal scores 0.5918 accuracy on that partition, higher than the accuracy of our best model.

\subsection{Dataset Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

The chain is implemented once. Every stage is switchable. That lets the ablation in
Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} change one thing at a time. The execution order is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Load and resample to 16~kHz mono.
\item Optionally, band-pass filter with a zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth between
50 and 2000~Hz.
\item Segment into cycles at the annotated start and end times.
\item Standardise every cycle to 8.0~s by cyclic tiling, Eq.~(\ref{eq:tile}).
\item Optionally normalize each cycle to unit peak amplitude.
\item Optionally denoise by spectral gating at the 25th percentile, with an
over-subtraction factor of two.
\item Compute a log-mel spectrogram, Eq.~(\ref{eq:logmel}), with 128 mel bands, a
1024-point FFT, hop 160, and window 400, over 50 to 2000~Hz.
\item Rescale each spectrogram to the unit interval, Eq.~(\ref{eq:minmax}).
\item Replicate to three channels for the pretrained backbones.
\item Encode the label, Eq.~(\ref{eq:label}).
\item Handle imbalance in the loss, Eq.~(\ref{eq:weights}).
\item Apply spectrogram masking, Eq.~(\ref{eq:specaug}), to the training split only.
\end{enumerate}

Three of those stages are off in the reference configuration and are done
deliberately. They are band-pass filtering, denoising, and per-cycle amplitude
normalization. Turning any of them on in the reference would silently move the
baseline that every other ablation delta is measured against. Instead, they are
switched on one at a time, as rows \texttt{P1}, \texttt{P2}, and \texttt{P3} of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Their deltas read in the opposite direction to
every other row: a positive delta is a recommendation to adopt the stage.

The median cycle is 2.42~s, against an 8.0~s input. Almost every cycle has to be
extended. We tile rather than pad with silence. For a cycle $x$ of length $L$, and a
target of $N = T f_s$ samples:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}[n] = x[n \bmod L], \qquad n = 0,\dots,N-1,
\label{eq:tile}
\end{equation}

A longer cycle is cropped from the start. Tiling is common practice here. It repeats
the same acoustic event two or three times within a single input. It leaves a
discontinuity at each seam. Row \texttt{P4} tests the alternative for reasons provided
in Section~\ref{subsec:xai}. The tiled waveform is converted to a log-mel spectrogram.
$X[m,k]$ is the short-time Fourier transform. $H_j[k]$ is the $j$-th mel filter:

\begin{equation}
S[j,m] = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\sum_{k} H_j[k]\,\lvert X[m,k]\rvert^{2}}
{\displaystyle\max_{j',m'}\ \sum_{k} H_{j'}[k]\,\lvert X[m',k]\rvert^{2}}\right).
\label{eq:logmel}
\end{equation}

The maximum is per spectrogram, not global. That is itself a normalisation. It is why
the explicit rescaling that follows is nearly free in the ablation. That rescaling is a
min--max map to the unit interval:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{S} = \frac{S - \min S}{\max S - \min S + \epsilon}, \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-8}.
\label{eq:minmax}
\end{equation}

Labels come from the two annotation flags: $c$ is the crackle flag, $w$ is the wheeze
flag, both in $\{0,1\}$:

\begin{equation}
y = c + 2w \in \{0,1,2,3\} \;\equiv\; \{\text{Normal},\ \text{Crackle},\ \text{Wheeze},\ \text{Both}\}.
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}

Instead of resampling, imbalance is addressed in the loss. This maintains the
identical training distribution across each model and ablation row. The raw
inverse-frequency weight for class $c$ is renormalised to the unit mean for $N_c$ of
$N$ training cycles and $C = 4$:

\begin{equation}
w_c = \frac{N}{C N_c} \bigg/ \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c'=1}^{C}\frac{N}{C N_{c'}},
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} w_{y_i}\log p_{i,y_i}.
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}

Without renormalisation, the loss scale moves with the class distribution. Ablation
rows that change the training set would no longer be comparable to the reference.
SpecAugment~\cite{park2019specaugment} is applied only to training spectrograms, using
the same seed as the paired clean run. The two runs differ in exactly one thing:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{S}[j,m] \leftarrow 0 \ \ \text{for}\ j \in [f_0, f_0{+}f),\ f \sim \mathcal{U}[0,24],
\ \ \text{and}\ m \in [t_0, t_0{+}t),\ t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,80],
\label{eq:specaug}
\end{equation}

Two frequency masks, two time masks, per spectrogram.

\subsection{Applied Models}
\label{subsec:models}
Table~\ref{tab:models} lists every model, with its owner. The four backbones trained under the corrected protocol share the same spectrograms, loss, partition, batch size of 16, cap of 40 epochs, and seed 42. Anything else would turn the comparison into a comparison of tuning effort. Only the classifier head is replaced. The two families do differ in optimizer settings. We state that rather than smoothing it over. The convolutional model employs Adam with a single learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$, dropout of 0.3, and gradient clipping of 5.0. Vision transformers use AdamW with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ on the backbone and $10^{-3}$ on the head, which is the conventional way to fine-tune a pretrained transformer. Both use cosine annealing and weight decay of $10^{-4}$. The ablation harness reuses the convolutional settings unchanged.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

The three abnormal classes are pooled against Normal in the ICBHI 2017 challenge
metric. The $4\times4$ confusion matrix is denoted $M$. The true class is represented
by rows. Normal is Index 0.

\begin{equation}
S_e = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3} M_{ii}}{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{3} M_{ij}},
\qquad
S_p = \frac{M_{00}}{\sum_{j=0}^{3} M_{0j}},
\qquad
\mathrm{ICBHI} = \frac{S_e + S_p}{2}.
\label{eq:official}
\end{equation}

A second definition circulates. It is the one our pipeline used before the audit. This
one is a macro average of per-class recall and per-class specificity:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ICBHI}_{\mathrm{macro}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(
\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\mathrm{Recall}_c +
\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\mathrm{Specificity}_c \right).
\label{eq:macro}
\end{equation}

The two are not close. Each per-class specificity in that macro formula counts true
negatives drawn from the other three classes. A rare class, such as Both, scores near
0.95 almost regardless of whether the model ever predicts it. Alongside the challenge
metric, we report accuracy, macro precision, macro recall, macro F1, parameters, size,
and training time for every model. Confidence intervals resample patients, not cycles,
2,000 times. Two configurations are compared by paired patient-level bootstrap. Where
per-cycle predictions exist for both, they are also compared by McNemar's test. The
concept-validity gate of Section~\ref{subsec:gate} was fixed before it was run. A
concept passes if:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUROC} \geq 0.65 \quad\text{and}\quad \text{the 95 percent DeLong interval excludes } 0.5,
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

The interval is computed by the DeLong method~\cite{delong1988auc}. The 0.65 floor is
part of the rule for a reason. An earlier version required only that the interval
exclude chance. At $n \approx 2{,}600$, that is satisfied at an AUROC of 0.52:
significant, and useless. A gate that cannot fail is not a gate.

The best score attained anywhere in this study was obtained using the physics-informed auxiliary loss. Consequently, the result of the low-rank adaptation should be understood in the context of its own experimental setup and not regarded as a definite overall winner.

Other experiments, including multistage distillation~\cite{pavel2024kd}, failed. The gated two-backbone fusion detector was very imbalanced and had a sensitivity of just 0.0932. \subsection{External Validation on SPRSound and HF\_Lung\_V1}
\label{subsec:external}

Every number reported so far describes how the pipeline behaves on ICBHI 2017 and says nothing
about whether it behaves that way anywhere else. This section runs the best checkpoint,
unchanged, over two corpora it has never seen and scores it with the same challenge metric
of~(\ref{eq:official}). No fine-tuning, no threshold tuning, and no target label is used to fit
anything in the headline column.

\textbf{Corpora.} SPRSound (BioCAS 2022) contributes 9,089 annotated events over 2,683
recordings from 288 pediatric patients, recorded at Shanghai Children's Medical Center on
Yunting Model II stethoscopes at 8~kHz. HF\_Lung\_V1 contributes 35,368 breath cycles over
9,765 adult recordings from Taiwan, on Littmann 3200 and HF\_Type-1 devices at 4~kHz. Both are
shifts in population \emph{and} in hardware. Neither uses the ICBHI label set: SPRSound events
carry seven types and HF\_Lung\_V1 carries onset/offset spans, so both are mapped onto the four
ICBHI classes, and an unmapped label string aborts the run rather than being bucketed into
Normal, which would silently inflate specificity.

\textbf{Verification gate.} Before any external number was computed, the checkpoint was
re-scored on the 2,636 ICBHI test cycles and required to reproduce the score stored inside it.
It returns 0.5588 against the stored 0.5602. The residual 0.0014 is about four cycles in 2,636
and is 10 times below the three-seed noise floor of 0.0141 established in
Section~\ref{subsec:stats}, whereas a structurally wrong forward path costs 0.05--0.30. Two
candidate causes were tested and rejected: the librosa version (one corpus was evaluated with
0.10.2 and the other with 0.11.0, both reproducing the same value) and the float16 quantisation
applied by the training cache (restoring it moved 0.5585 to 0.5588). The remainder is
unexplained, and the external columns below are read against the reproduced 0.5588 rather than
the stored value so that both sides of every difference come from one verified forward pass.

\textbf{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:external} gives the transfer scores with the trivial baselines
that make them readable. The always-Normal predictor scores exactly 0.50 on this metric by
construction, and the prior-matched random predictor draws from the target class distribution,
so beating it is what distinguishes a model that knows which cycle is which from one that has
merely learned how common each class is.

On SPRSound events the model reaches 0.4008 [0.374, 0.427]. That is below the always-Normal
floor and also below prior-matched random at 0.4392, so on this corpus the frozen model is not
shown to carry usable class information at all. The confusion matrix locates the failure
precisely: 5,097 of 6,887 true Normal events are predicted Crackle, and specificity collapses
from 0.712 in-domain to 0.246. On HF\_Lung\_V1 the score is 0.4933 [0.477, 0.511], an interval
that contains 0.50, so the model is not shown to beat the floor there either, though it does
clear prior-matched random. The record-level SPRSound row is reported for completeness and is
the weakest of the three: every record is longer than the 8~s input window, so the model sees
only the first eight seconds of a recording whose label describes the whole of it.

Three arms were computed to close the objections a single transfer score invites. The
\emph{strict} arm drops every row whose label depends on a rhonchi or stridor annotation, since
ICBHI never annotated a low-pitched continuous sound or an upper-airway inspiratory one; it
moves SPRSound by 0.0017 and HF\_Lung\_V1 by 0.0065, so the one judgement call in the taxonomy
mapping is priced at less than half the noise floor. The \emph{prior-corrected} arm re-decides
each cycle after exchanging the training class prior for the target's; it is a diagnostic
rather than a zero-shot number because it uses the target labels, and it moves SPRSound from
0.4008 to 0.4161 and HF\_Lung\_V1 by 0.0004. Class-balance shift therefore accounts for at most
a sixth of the SPRSound drop and none of the HF\_Lung\_V1 drop. Every interval resamples
patients for SPRSound and recording sessions for HF\_Lung\_V1, the latter because the corpus
carries no patient identifier; calling it patient-level would overstate what the resampling
controls for.

\textbf{What transfers, and what does not.} The frozen-feature probe separates the two
possible explanations. A logistic head fitted on the frozen backbone under grouped five-fold
cross-validation reaches \best{0.7363} [0.714, 0.756] on SPRSound, which is higher than the
same backbone's own in-domain score. The representation therefore transfers; what fails is the
ICBHI-fitted decision boundary. On HF\_Lung\_V1 the probe reaches only 0.4871 [0.467, 0.507],
indistinguishable from the zero-shot number, so there the representation itself carries little
usable information about the corpus. The 4~kHz sampling rate is a candidate explanation:
Nyquist sits at 2,000~Hz, exactly the model's mel \texttt{fmax}, so the top of the analysis band
arrives attenuated by the recorder's anti-aliasing filter. The two corpora thus fail for
different reasons, and only the probe distinguishes them.

The detect-only rescoring behaves differently again. Collapsing the four classes to
abnormal-versus-normal gives 0.6211 on HF\_Lung\_V1 against 0.6332 in-domain, a drop of 0.0121:
the ability to tell a normal cycle from an abnormal one survives the move to adult Taiwanese
recordings almost intact, and only sub-typing collapses. On SPRSound the same rescoring gives
0.4357, so there the failure is not confined to sub-typing.

\subsection{Calibration under Distribution Shift}
\label{subsec:calibration}

A model that fails while remaining confident is worse than one that fails visibly.
Table~\ref{tab:trust} reports expected calibration error, mean maximum softmax probability and
overconfidence, defined as mean confidence minus accuracy, on each corpus.

The pattern is consistent and it runs the wrong way. Confidence \emph{rises} as accuracy falls.
On ICBHI the model is already poorly calibrated, with an ECE of 0.3039 at 0.5869 accuracy. On
SPRSound events it reports a mean confidence of 0.9351 while achieving 0.3208 accuracy, an
overconfidence of 0.6142 and an ECE of 0.6143. Nearly 80 percent of SPRSound events receive a
confidence above 0.90. Whatever the network has learned to represent, the softmax margin it
produces carries no information about whether the corpus is one it can handle.

\subsection{A Far-Out-of-Distribution Negative Control on CirCor DigiScope}
\label{subsec:negcontrol}

CirCor DigiScope is a phonocardiogram corpus: it contains heart sounds, annotated for murmur
presence. A crackle-and-wheeze classifier has no label overlap with that annotation, so scoring
it under~(\ref{eq:official}) would yield no generalization number, and it is not treated as a
transfer corpus here. No CirCor label is read at any point. The corpus enters as one thing
only, audio that is by definition not a respiratory cycle, which makes it a negative control on
the two questions the transfer scores cannot answer: whether the model can tell that an input
is outside its domain, and what it claims when it cannot.

The public release supplies 3,163 recordings from 942 patients, cut into 10,172 windows of
8~s. The known side is the same 2,636 ICBHI test cycles the verification gate gives, so both
sides come from one verified forward pass. Rejection is scored by the post-hoc energy score
$-\log\sum_c e^{z_c}$ on the frozen backbone, the statistic already used for the near-out-of-distribution
baseline of Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}, with maximum softmax probability and
predictive entropy reported beside it and intervals that resample patients on both sides.

The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:trust} gives the outcome. None of the three scores separates
heart sounds from lung sounds: the energy AUROC is 0.4942 [0.449, 0.544], and the maximum
softmax and entropy AUROCs are 0.4809 and 0.4778, every interval containing 0.50. The model
assigns a mean confidence of 0.9051 to heart sounds, \emph{higher} than the 0.8905 it assigns
to its own in-domain test cycles, with 40.4 percent of heart-sound windows above 0.99 against
37.5 percent of ICBHI cycles. It labels 23.2 percent of them as pathological lung sounds.

This result must be read against the difficulty of the task rather than as an improvement on
the near-out-of-distribution number. Rejecting a heartbeat is easier than rejecting an unseen
lung pathology, so the 0.6466 of Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}, measured over 19 unknown
patients, is not superseded by anything here. The value of the control is the opposite: on the
easiest available version of the task, with 942 patients rather than 19 and intervals roughly
0.10 wide, the detector is at chance. The earlier result was reported as ``not shown to beat''
because the sample was too small to support a stronger claim; this one is a well-powered null,
and it makes the case that the open-set mechanism works substantially weaker rather than
stronger.

One mechanism is identifiable from the pipeline itself. The preprocessing of
Section~\ref{subsec:preprocessing} maps every spectrogram to $[0,1]$ by per-input min--max
normalisation, so absolute level, recording gain and microphone sensitivity are removed by
construction from every input regardless of its origin. An energy-based detector reads logits
produced from scale-free input and therefore cannot use loudness to notice that audio is alien;
only shape within the 50--2000~Hz band remains available to it. How much of that shape in fact
separates a heartbeat from a breath is not measured here, so this is offered as a named
mechanism and a limitation rather than as a demonstrated cause.

\subsection{Limitations of the External Evaluation}
\label{subsec:extlimits}

Four limitations bound the claims above. First, the verification gate reproduces 0.5588 rather
than the stored 0.5602, and although the gap is an order of magnitude below the seed noise
floor and two candidate causes were eliminated, it is not explained. Second, HF\_Lung\_V1
provides no patient identifier, so its intervals resample recording sessions and control for
less than the SPRSound intervals do; its 4~kHz sampling also places Nyquist exactly at the
model's upper mel bound, which is a confound with the domain shift rather than a separable
factor. Third, the HF\_Lung\_V1 evaluation covers the whole corpus rather than the published
test split, which widens the sample but makes the number not directly comparable to work
reporting on that split. Fourth, the CirCor control establishes only that a
far-out-of-distribution input is not rejected; it cannot establish how the detector would behave on the
near-out-of-distribution cases that matter clinically, which remain limited by the 19-patient
unknown group.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}Our goal was to create an interpretable, open-world respiratory sound classifier, but establishing an evaluation protocol proved more difficult. Our concept detectors reached 0.5580 and 0.5340 in two runs using the pre-specified stopping rule for the labels ICBHI crackle and wheeze. This prompted a closer look at the evaluation pipeline, resulting in a fully corrected protocol. According to this protocol, the previously reported score of 0.7077 is reduced to 0.5602, which is 0.1475 more than the effects of the architectural options studied.

The best model we have is MobileNetV2 with spectrogram masking at 0.5602 (corrected partition) / 0.5641 (published partition) with 2.2~M parameters. All three vision transformers failed to reach it, and ViT-B/16 even collapsed towards the majority class, despite having around 38 times as many parameters. The audio-pretrained transformer is not directly comparable, as it predates the audit and lacks a stored prediction table. These ablation results show that fine-tuning makes a significant contribution compared to pretraining; frozen ImageNet features on log-mel input are inferior to fine-tuning; masking mainly improves specificity; and class weighting does not contribute as much as the padding strategy. Several of these conclusions are weakened by the fact that the three-seed noise floor of 0.0141 is quite high, and one conclusion is based on a patient-level test of 47 patients.

The end result of the concept branch was more informative. Interestingly, our detectors performed poorly on the same evaluation cycles, whereas a detector pre-registered with the frozen AudioSet embeddings achieved scores of 0.7115 and 0.7621, indicating that the labels themselves can still be detected and that the problem lay with the extractors we chose. The label produced by the resulting machine system on the same 132 clips was closer to ICBHI wheeze labels than a self-consistent physician (differences 0.169, $p=0.046$).

The simple recommendation is to report the challenge metric together with sensitivity and specificity, specify the evaluation partition and its patient independence, keep the original confusion matrix, explain the selection rule for checkpoints, and assess statistical claims at the patient level. These practices ensure that results are more easily repeatable, understood, and compared across systems.

Three directions follow. A clean validation protocol and a rerun of the fourth transformer would eliminate the current test-partition selection advantage and place all models in the same test environment. Second, a task-specific reference standard (calibrated by multi-rater re-annotation, and with an explicit ``fits no named category'' option) might be useful for separating model error from label uncertainty. Third, the fact that fine-tuning strategies that preserve useful representations from large audio-pretrained models offer a promising path forward provides a concrete scale for measuring future adaptation strategies: the frozen probe recovers the target labels at 0.71--0.76 without trainable adaptation, offering a concrete basis for developing future strategies.

\begin{addedblock}
The external evaluation added after the first draft sharpens rather than softens this reading.
Moved to SPRSound and HF\_Lung\_V1 without any change to its weights, the best model scores
0.4008 and 0.4933 against an always-Normal floor of 0.50, and on SPRSound it also falls below a
prior-matched random predictor. A logistic probe on the same frozen features reaches 0.7363 on
SPRSound, so the representation survives the move and the ICBHI-fitted decision boundary does
not. The corpus therefore moves the score by more than any architectural choice examined here,
which is the paper's central claim measured once more on data we did not choose. Two further
observations bear on trustworthiness: confidence rises as accuracy falls, reaching a mean of
0.9351 at 0.3208 accuracy on SPRSound, and on a phonocardiogram corpus used as a negative
control the model neither rejects heart sounds at better than chance nor becomes less confident
about them than about its own test cycles. The open-set mechanism is therefore weaker than the
19-patient result alone suggested, not stronger.
\end{addedblock}

This work is based on two principles: setting the decision threshold prior to the experiment and testing the evaluation protocol's validity before drawing architectural conclusions.
